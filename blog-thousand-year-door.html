Returning to the Thousand-Year Door

Paper Mario: The Thousand-Year Door is my favorite game, although saying I played it as a kid is not completely accurate. I got a GameCube for my fourth birthday, and I was definitely too young to make it very far by myself. My dad was the one actually playing. I would sit next to him, follow along, and feel like I was part of it anyway.

I remember the story, the music, the characters, and all the different places more than I remember any one play session. A lot of it probably went straight over my head at four years old, but I still got attached to it. Rogueport and the Crystal Stars felt familiar long before I ever understood the whole story. Most of my earliest memories of the game are really memories of watching it with my dad.

When I got older and wanted to play the whole thing myself, I ended up using the Dolphin emulator and ISO files on a computer. It was not the original setup, but I finally got to go through the game at my own pace. I could explore everything, figure out the battles for myself, and actually understand what everyone was talking about. It felt weirdly new even though I had already watched so much of it.

I've been collecting a lot more games and hardware recently, so I finally pulled the GameCube back out and set everything up. Then I played through The Thousand-Year Door again on the actual console. Using the original controller and memory card made the whole thing feel different from emulation. Maybe that is a little sentimental, but finishing it that way felt like closing a loop that started with me sitting there watching my dad play.

And honestly, the game still holds up. The story is funny and strange, but it also has moments that hit harder than I remembered. The soundtrack is ridiculously good. I can hear certain songs and immediately picture where I was in the game. I love the partners, the badges, the timed button presses during battles, the audience reacting in the background, and all the random stage effects. There is just so much personality packed into it.

I also think it is really cool that there is still a community dedicated to speedrunning it. People are still working on routes, finding tricks, and figuring out ways to save time in a GameCube game from 2004. JakeBop is my favorite person to watch when he plays it. A speedrun makes a game I know really well look almost unrecognizable, and I like seeing how excited the community still gets when something new is found.

I really wish there were more games like it. There are other Paper Mario games I enjoy and plenty of great RPGs, but none of them feel exactly the same to me. Some of that is obviously nostalgia. It would be impossible to separate the game from getting that GameCube, watching my dad, and eventually coming back to finish it myself. But I also replayed it as an adult and still loved basically everything about it. So yes, nostalgia is part of it. The game is also just that good.
